## 0. The short version

The drums are underwhelming for one root cause and eight compounding ones.
The root cause is that every drum in the app is **synthesised from sine
waves and filtered noise**. The eight compounding ones are things a real
drum backing track has and this one does not: velocity layers, round
robins, hat choke, ringing tails, humanised dynamics, a drum bus, stereo,
and grooves written with a drummer's dynamics. Better synthesis will not
close the gap; a decade of drum machines proves that a sine-and-noise kit
sounds like a drum machine however carefully it is tuned. **Recorded
samples close it**, the engine already has half the plumbing (a folder of
WAVs becomes a kit), and there are recorded kits under licences that let
a free app ship them. The recommendation in §5 is one route, in order,
with what it costs.

## 1. What the engine plays today (the facts)

Read from `src-tauri/src/engine.rs`, `jam.rs`, `kit.rs`,
`src-tauri/sounds/KITS.md` and `src/jam/compile.ts`.

| | Today | A drum backing track people enjoy |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Sines and noise, generated by a script, deterministic | A drummer, recorded |
| Samples per voice | **1** | 3–6 velocity layers × 2–4 round robins |
| How "soft" is made | The same file, quieter (ghost 0.35, hit 0.8, accent 1.0) | A different hit: soft strokes have less crack, more wood |
| Repetition | Every hit bit-identical | Never the same hit twice |
| Hat behaviour | Open hat rings on under the next closed hat | A closed hat **chokes** the open one |
| Tails | Crash 600 ms, ride 300 ms, kick 150 ms | Crash 2–4 s, ride 1–2 s, kick 300–500 ms |
| Timing and level | Exact grid, exact gain | Small, musical drift: ±2–3 % level, a few ms of push and pull |
| Mix | Dry hits summed, mono, no processing | Overheads and room, a bus with glue compression and a little saturation, stereo |
| Grooves | 3 levels per cell; hats flat | Hats alternating strong/weak, ghost notes under the backbeat, crash tails over the bar line |
| Levels | Normalised to peak 0.9, balance tuned for a laptop speaker band-pass | Balanced by ear on the drums' own character |

Two design choices made the first two rows what they are, and both were
mine: "everything synthesised, nothing to download" (a Jam principle in
`JAM_MODE.md`) and "the kit must fit in 600 KB". They bought a 8 MB
installer and a deterministic engine. They cost the sound. The principle
was right about *not making the user download anything*; it was wrong to
conclude that the app cannot carry a recording inside itself.

## 2. Why each gap matters, in the order you hear it

1. **Synthesis.** A snare is a drum head, a shell, twenty snare wires and a
   room, excited by a stick. Sines and noise reproduce the envelope, not
   the object. The ear tells them apart in one hit. The Raw kit is the
   best a day of synthesis produced; the owner's verdict on it stands.
2. **One sample, scaled.** A real ghost note is a different sound from a
   backbeat, not a quiet copy. With one file, soft and loud are the same
   colour, which is the "flat" feeling under every groove.
3. **No round robins.** Identical hits in a row produce the machine-gun
   effect; the brain flags the repetition within two bars. Three
   alternates per layer are enough to stop it.
4. **No choke.** A drummer's open hat is closed by the next foot or stick;
   here it rings across, smearing every off-beat open hat into the next
   beat. Loud intensity uses open hats heavily, so Loud is where it is
   worst.
5. **Short tails.** A crash that stops at 600 ms is the loudest tell in the
   kit: crashes *are* their decay. Ride at 300 ms is a tap.
6. **No drift.** Exact gain on an exact grid is what "programmed" sounds
   like. A seeded ±2 % gain and ±2 ms micro-timing per hit, re-seeded per
   bar so it never loops audibly, reads as a person.
7. **No bus.** Dry hits summed are thin. Glue compression makes the kit
   move as one thing; light saturation adds the "recorded" edge; a short
   room puts the drummer in a space. This is also where "raw" versus
   "smooth" is finally decided.
8. **Mono.** Overheads left and right are most of what "a kit in a room"
   means on headphones.
9. **Flat grooves.** Even with a perfect kit, a hat row of identical accents
   is not a drummer. Grooves need the strong/weak alternation on hats, a
   couple of ghost notes, and fills whose hits get louder toward the crash.

## 3. Routes

### A. Better synthesis
Physical modelling of membranes and cymbals is a research field, not a
day's work, and the best commercial results still sit below a decent
recording for acoustic drums. **Rejected as the main path.** Keep it for
the Electronic kit, where a synthesised 808 kick is the real thing.

### B. Recorded kits, shipped inside the app
Take a recorded multi-mic, multi-velocity drum library under a licence
that allows redistribution, render each hit down to the app's format (a
stereo mix of close mics and overheads, at four velocity layers and three
round robins per voice, tails kept), and embed it as today's kits are
embedded. Candidates found, with licences read from their own pages:

| Library | Licence | What it is | Raw size | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Virtuosity Drums** (Versilian Studios + Karoryfer, KVRDC 2021) | **CC0 1.0** | Full acoustic kit recorded by drummer Austin McMahon in Boston; kick, snare (on/off, centre, rimshot, cross-stick, flam), toms, hi-hat, ride, flat ride, crashes; six mixable mic positions; SFZ | ~1.2 GB repo | Public domain: no attribution needed. The obvious rock and pop kit. |
| **DRSKit** (DrumGizmo project) | **CC BY 4.0** | Kick, toms, snare, Paiste hats, two crashes, ride; 13 mic channels; "from jazz to rock" | 2.8 GB zip | Attribution line in the app's About. |
| **CrocellKit** (DrumGizmo project) | **CC BY 4.0** | A metal band's studio kit, 15 channels, double kick, chinas and splashes | 5.5 GB | Attribution. The Metal vibe's kit. |
| **Salamander Drumkit** (Alexander Holm) | **CC BY-SA 3.0** | SFZ-mapped acoustic kit | 370 MB | Share-alike: the rendered files stay BY-SA. Workable for a free app, but CC0 is simpler. |

Rendering keeps only what the engine plays: eight voices (plus toms for
fills, a ninth and tenth voice worth adding), four layers, three round
robins, trimmed to musical lengths. Estimate per kit at 48 kHz, 16-bit,
stereo: about 100 hits × 0.4 s average with cymbals longer → **10–20 MB
per kit** inside the installer, against today's 8 MB total. Two recorded
kits roughly triple the installer. That is the cost the owner has to
accept; it is small by the standards of any music app and it removes the
download question entirely.

### C. The engine
Needed whichever recordings are used, and it is what makes the owner's
own sample folders sound right too:

1. **Banks with layers and round robins.** A voice is `[layer][rr]` rather
   than one buffer; the layer is chosen by the hit's level (ghost → 1,
   hit → 2, accent → 3, fill peak → 4), the round robin cycles. Custom
   folders learn the same shape: `snare_1.wav … snare_4.wav` and
   `snare_1_rr2.wav`, with today's eight plain names still working.
2. **Choke groups.** Closed hat and pedal choke the open hat with a 20 ms
   fade; a cymbal choke on demand. One rule on the audio thread, no
   allocation.
3. **Humanise.** Seeded per bar from the bar number: ±2 % gain, ±2 ms
   timing on non-downbeats, never on the kick's downbeat. Deterministic,
   so a take is reproducible; varied, so it never loops.
4. **A drum bus.** Stereo, a soft-knee compressor with 30 ms attack, a
   tanh stage for the raw kits, and a short stereo room from the
   recordings' own room mics (already in the samples) rather than a
   reverb. All fixed-cost DSP, measured on the jitter probe like
   everything else.
5. **Stereo output for the band.** The click stays where it is.

### D. The grooves
Rewrite the twenty grooves with dynamics: hat alternation (accent / hit /
ghost across eighths and sixteenths), ghost notes under the backbeat in
the grooves that have them (funk, shuffle, half-time), crash tails
allowed to ring over the bar line, fills that ramp. The five levels above
(off, ghost, hit, accent, peak) instead of four. This is data work in
`src/jam/grooves.ts` and it matters as much as the samples.

### E. The owner's own drums, sooner
Everything in B–D also serves the folder-of-WAVs path. A player with a
sample pack they like should get layers and round robins from it by
naming files; that is the fastest way anyone hears a band in this app,
today, without a decision about installer size.

## 4. What it costs

| Step | Work | Who |
|---|---|---|
| Render tool: SFZ/DrumGizmo → the app's bank format (stereo mix, layers, RRs, trims, loudness match) | 1 day | one worker, Python |
| Engine: layered banks, choke, humanise, stereo band, bus | 1–2 days | one worker, Rust, probe-gated |
| Grooves rewritten with dynamics, five levels | 1 day | one worker, TypeScript |
| Kits: Virtuosity Drums as the rock/pop kit; a second kit (DRSKit or CrocellKit) once the first is heard | ½ day each | the render tool |
| Listening rounds with the owner | as many as it takes | the owner |

Three workers in parallel, then the kits: two to three days to a first
recorded kit playing in the app, on branch `jam-v2` or its successor.

## 5. Recommendation

Do B + C + D, in that order of dependency, starting with **Virtuosity
Drums (CC0)** as the kit for Rock, Hard rock, Pop, Country and Blues, the
Electronic kit kept synthesised, and the four synthesised acoustic kits
(Room, Tight, Brushes, Raw) retired once the recorded one is heard, so
the kit list stops offering four flavours of the same disappointment.
Brushes come back as a recorded kit when a licensed one turns up. Metal
gets CrocellKit when its licence line is in the About screen.

The installer grows from 8 MB to roughly 25–40 MB. The app still downloads
nothing at runtime, which is the promise that mattered.
